MICROPLASTICS(MPs)

By Christina Brenckman • August 11, 2026

Microplastics are tiny plastic particles less than 5 millimeters in size that are now found throughout the environment, including oceans, rivers, soil, air, food, and drinking water. Some are intentionally manufactured at a small size, while others form when larger plastic products break down over time. Their widespread presence has raised concerns because microplastics can persist in the environment, be consumed by wildlife, carry other pollutants, and potentially enter the human body through food, water, and air. Although research has greatly improved our understanding of where microplastics come from and how they move through the environment, important questions remain about their long-term effects on ecosystems and human health. Understanding both what scientists know and what remains uncertain is essential for determining the true risks of microplastic pollution and developing effective solutions.

Background

Microplastics are plastic particles smaller than 5 millimeters that have become widespread contaminants in the environment. They can be intentionally produced at a small size or form when larger plastic items, such as bottles, bags, packaging, and fishing gear, break down through sunlight, weathering, and physical abrasion. Other common sources include synthetic clothing fibers, tire wear particles, and industrial materials. Once released, microplastics can enter waterways, soil, and the atmosphere, allowing them to travel far from their original sources. The environmental concern surrounding microplastics comes largely from their persistence and widespread distribution. Plastic does not readily biodegrade, meaning these particles can remain in the environment for long periods and continue breaking into increasingly smaller pieces. Microplastics have been detected in marine and freshwater environments, wildlife, food, drinking water, and air. Their small size also makes them available for ingestion by many organisms and creates opportunities for interaction with other environmental contaminants. While scientists have established that microplastic pollution is widespread, questions remain about its long-term ecological effects, human health implications, and the most effective ways to reduce exposure and environmental contamination.

Why it matters

Microplastic pollution matters because these particles are persistent, widespread, and difficult to remove once they enter the environment. Wildlife can ingest microplastics, potentially affecting feeding, growth, reproduction, and overall health. Microplastics can also interact with chemicals and other pollutants, creating additional concerns about their effects on ecosystems. Humans are exposed to microplastics through food, drinking water, and the air, although the long-term health effects of this exposure are still being studied. As plastic production and waste continue to increase, understanding the risks associated with microplastics is important for protecting ecosystems, human health, and water quality and for developing effective strategies to reduce plastic pollution.

Final thoughts

Microplastics have become a part of our everyday lives, often without us even realizing it. They are found in the water we drink, the food we eat, the air we breathe, and many of the products and materials we use every day. Despite how widespread they are, many people are still unaware of how frequently they may encounter microplastics or where they come from. While scientists are continuing to study their long-term effects on human health and the environment, their presence nearly everywhere makes microplastic pollution an issue that should not be ignored. Greater awareness, continued research, and efforts to reduce unnecessary plastic use are important steps toward understanding and addressing this growing environmental problem.
